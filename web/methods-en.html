PapaMap — methods & honesty

Changing tables dads can reach. The worldwide taginfo numbers below come from the nightly build. The sample across eight city cores is from 26 Jul 2026 and stays that way.

What this map is. And what it is not.

PapaMap shows places in Germany and Denmark where OpenStreetMap knows about a baby changing table (changing_table=yes or limited). The color says whether a dad can reach the table. The map reads OSM and nothing else, and it writes nowhere. Fixing the data happens on OSM itself, through the links on each pin.

A ranking of which city has the best toilet provision this is not. Why that can't honestly be done from open data is explained further down.

What the colors mean (the exact rule)

Only objects with changing_table=yes or limited make it onto the map (no only counts in the stats). The color comes from changing_table:location. The value is split on ;, trimmed, lowercased and compared as a whole token. Never as a substring, because female_toilet contains male.

  • Green: one token is male_toilet, unisex_toilet, dedicated_room, room, wheelchair_toilet or sales_area. A dad can get to it.
  • Red: otherwise, if one token is female_toilet. The table exists, but only in the women's room.
  • Grey: everything else. No location tag, or only free text. Nobody has recorded the room. The grey pins are the job.

One exception: objects with a centralkey tag (other than no) are not shown at all. The Euro key and similar central key systems are only issued against proof of disability — whatever room the table is in, most dads don't get through that door.

The blue ring: play area

A pin with a blue ring also has a play area — a place you don't just change the kid, you stay a while. The With play area chip filters the map down to those. A place counts if OSM records any one of these:

  • kids_area:indoor=yes or designated — the wiki's documented way of saying indoors.
  • kids_area=yes, indoor or designated — the play corner in a café, a restaurant, a bakery. The most-used tag, though it leaves indoor or outdoor open.
  • leisure=indoor_play — a commercial indoor playground.
  • leisure=playground together with indoor=yes — the same thing, tagged the other way round.

outdoor does not count: a beer garden's sandpit is not what the ring promises. Neither does no, nor limited — the wiki defines that one as "toys are available, but no designated area". Where an explicit kids_area:indoor=no is present, it beats a bare kids_area=yes.

This is a badge, not a fourth status. A place without these tags does not "have no play area" — OSM simply says nothing about it. So there is no grey here and no call to action: with changing_table:location the silence is meaningful, because the table is known to exist. Here almost every object is silent, and that means nothing. If you know a play area, adding it is still welcome — in the OSM editor, as kids_area=yes.

The empty blue rings: places to play, no answer

The same rule matches far more places than the map has pins. In Germany and Denmark 828 objects pass it — but only 111 of them carry a changing_table tag at all. For the other 701 nobody has answered the first question: is there a changing table here? Yes, no, nobody knows — OSM says nothing whatsoever.

The map cannot colour those 701, because there is nothing to colour. They are not grey either: grey means "the table exists, the room is missing", and that does not apply here. So they get their own chip, Play area only, off by default, and an empty blue ring instead of a filled pin.

They are the most promising open questions on the whole map. An indoor playground, a café with a ball pit, a restaurant with a play room — a father with a toddler is going there anyway, and once he is there, he knows the answer. Clicking a ring opens MapComplete on exactly that one question. Answer it and the ring is gone at the next nightly refresh, replaced by a real pin.

How to fix a grey pin

Grey means one answer is missing. Where is the changing table? Anyone with a free OSM account can answer that, on site, in under a minute:

  • MapComplete (any phone, in the browser): “Answer on MapComplete” on a grey pin opens the same object in PapaMap's own MapComplete theme — every pin, so the edits are recognizable as coming from here. MapComplete warns once about an unofficial theme, that's fine. Log in with your OSM account and answer the changing-table questions, including the one about the room. The answer goes straight into OpenStreetMap and shows up here after the next nightly update.
  • StreetComplete (Android): has a changing-table quest, off by default. Go to Settings → Quest selection & priority, search for “changing table” and tick it. It only asks yes/no. For the room question on an existing grey pin, use MapComplete.

The edits run under your own OSM account and belong to everyone. They outlive this website and help every app that reads OpenStreetMap.

How to add a missing place

PapaMap stores nothing itself. Adding a place therefore always means: editing OpenStreetMap. The + Add a place button on the map opens the right editor at your current view.

A public toilet is missing entirely

Take PapaMap's MapComplete theme (the first link in the dialog). Log in, tap the map where the toilet is, pick “a public toilet”, answer the questions. Including the ones about the changing table and the room.

A café, shop or restaurant has a table nobody mapped

The place almost certainly exists in OSM already. Only the changing_table tag is missing. In OSM's own editor (iD) that takes about two minutes:

  1. Create a free account on openstreetmap.org (one email is enough) and log in.
  2. Find the place and click Edit. The place is a point or a building outline. Click it, its tags appear on the left.
  3. Expand All tags at the bottom and add with +:
    • changing_table = yes
    • changing_table:location = where it is, from the documented value list: male_toilet, female_toilet, unisex_toilet, wheelchair_toilet, dedicated_room, room or sales_area. In several rooms: separate with semicolons (male_toilet;female_toilet).
  4. Press Save, write a short comment (“add changing table location”), upload. Live on OSM within a minute, on PapaMap after the next nightly update.

Please only tag what you have seen yourself. OSM records what is, not what should be. If you don't know the room, changing_table=yes alone is still useful: the place becomes a grey pin, and the next dad answers the room question.

Why a provision scorecard from OSM doesn't work

The obvious bigger project would be a male-to-female toilet provision ratio per city. The number the “potty parity” argument actually turns on. It cannot be computed from OpenStreetMap. We checked before building, with a sample across eight city cores (Berlin, London, Paris, New York, Tokyo 23 wards, Amsterdam, Vienna, Melbourne; 26 Jul 2026):

  • Only 29.9% of mapped toilets carry any gender tag at all. Both (male and female): 8.5%.
  • The tags are pure yes/no flags, never counts. Cubicle counts per gender: zero in all eight cities. The one capacity key, toilets:num_chambers (~27k uses), is ungendered and comes almost entirely from an old import. Its variants :female / :male have 2 uses each worldwide.
  • Hamburg, checked the same day: 443 mapped toilets, none with a toilets:num_chambers* tag. The strip on the front page recomputes these numbers nightly.
  • And: tagging rates measure the mapper community, not the cities. Single-occupancy toilets correctly stay ungendered. A city ranking would grade OSM contributors, not toilet equity.

So the only honest statement is: provision itself cannot be measured from open data today. This site measures what is measurable and turns the gaps into contributions.

The one computable fact: where the tables are

changing_table:location is, as far as we know, the only open vocabulary that records which room a changing table is in. It is sparse: 3,659 of 77,000 changing_table objects worldwide, 4.8%. But where it exists, it is lopsided (taginfo, 26 Jul 2026):

  • Tables only in the women's room: 485.
  • Tables only in the men's room: 71. Roughly 7:1. 289 are tagged in both rooms, 410 record any men's-room table at all. Worldwide.

The sample is small, and mappers choose what they record. So don't over-read the exact ratio. The direction matches what has long been documented elsewhere: changing tables hang in the women's room by default. That locks dads out, and it quietly assumes who is in charge of diapers. This map makes the missing data visible. And easy to fix.

If you follow a link to MapComplete, StreetComplete or openstreetmap.org, you are on their sites, editing under your own OSM account. Nothing passes through us.
